Crisis or Reinvention? German Industry, the Energy Transition, and the Case of Mecklenburg-Vorpommern

Many headlines describe the German industry as being in a deep and possibly terminal crisis. Often, these articles claim the larger statistics, such as a declining GDP, or emphasize Germany's decline in sectors such as automotive, machinery, and chemicals. This essay argues that these headlines calling Germany a country in crisis are likely a pessimistic and short-sighted perspective, mistaking a difficult and infrastructure-bound reinvention for an actual decline. The very foundations of the German economy are changing, and with those changes the headline statistics could stand as misleading indicators of Germany's future. Using Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, a region often perceived as one of economic decline, this essay endeavors to examine the German economy transforming under the pressure of many influences. Specifically, the Baltic village of Lubmin near Greifswald can show how a coastline that once symbolised German dependence on Russian gas became a demonstration of how quickly the country can adapt under pressure. By weighing the pessimistic indicators against strengths that are often overlooked in the energy transition, digitalization, and changing global trade patterns, I argue that the constraints on economic resilience are physical infrastructure and social cohesion rather than engineering ability. Therefore, companies and policymakers should prioritise accordingly. On the whole, German industry is not in a straightforward decline; it is in a contested reinvention whose outcome remains genuinely open.

Introduction

When The Economist revived the label "the sick man of Europe" for Germany, it captured a widely shared anxiety. The magazine reminded readers that "nearly twenty-five years ago this newspaper called Germany the sick man of the euro," and warned that, while the country had since prospered, "the world has kept on turning" and "Germany has once again started to fall behind" (The Economist, 2023). The three pillars of the postwar industrial model, which were inexpensive energy, open global trade, and dominance in mechanical engineering, all became unstable at nearly the same time. Yet alarm and adaptation can occur together, and a gloomy national average can conceal the adaptive behaviour that is only visible at the regional level. Nowhere is this clearer than on the Baltic coast of Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, where the Nord Stream pipelines made landfall at Lubmin. Within months of the 2022 supply shock, the very site that had symbolised German dependence on Russian gas hosted the country's first privately financed liquefied natural gas (LNG) import terminal, built with roughly 100 million euros of the operator's own capital and no state financing (NDR, 2023; Bundesnetzagentur, 2023). Despite Mecklenburg-Vorpommern's reputation as being an area representing Germany's economic decline and a region so often treated as peripheral, it in fact sits at the centre of this national story and represents the potential of economic reprise as a region known for a history of economic decline.

This essay addresses the central question of whether German industry is genuinely in decline, or whether it is being reinvented. In this context, 'decline' constitutes a broad and lasting loss of industrial capacity, while 'reinvention' constitutes a shift of that same prior capacity onto new energy, digital, and trade foundations. In order to determine the state of German industry, I will examine the indicators that support the pessimistic view, the developments (such as those in the energy transition, digitalization, and global trade) that point to overlooked strengths, and the strategic priorities that follow for companies and policymakers. This essay endeavors to inspect each of these indicators through technical, economic, and social perspectives. For scope and focus, I will primarily analyse Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, even though other regions in Germany share these pressures, because it is a region that brings these dimensions together.

Overall, I argue in this essay that German industry can not be described as being in a simple decline but rather in a forced reinvention. The most effective response is to treat physical infrastructure and social cohesion as the constraints, rather than having a focus on aspects like engineering capacity. This paper explores how the example of Lubmin's pivot demonstrates how Germany can adapt quickly when it must. It is an essay that also endeavors to explain how that capacity can be sustained over time, given that the grid, the price of industrial energy, digital adoption, and the trust of communities such as those in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern are addressed together.

Factors on Germany's Decline

One of the primary reasons people believe German industry is in decline is that the headline indicators look poor. German GDP contracted in two consecutive years, by 0.3 percent in 2023 and by a further 0.2 percent in 2024, the worst performance among large advanced economies, and the IMF projected that Germany would be the only major economy to shrink in 2023 (Statistisches Bundesamt, 2025; Deutsche Welle, 2024). The Federal Statistical Office attributed the result to deep-seated problems rather than a passing dip, noting that "cyclical and structural pressures stood in the way of better economic development," including "increasing competition for the German export industry on key sales markets, high energy costs, [and] an interest rate level that remains high" (Statistisches Bundesamt, 2025). Take, for example, the automotive sector. In September 2024, Volkswagen openly considered closing plants in Germany for the first time in the company's 87-year history. Group CEO Oliver Blume warned that "the economic environment has become even tougher and new players are pushing into Europe," adding that "Germany as a business location is falling further behind in terms of competitiveness" (Bloomberg, 2024). The brand chief, Thomas Schäfer, was blunter still, stating that "the situation is extremely tense and cannot be resolved through simple cost-cutting measures" (CNBC, 2024). In chemicals, BASF reduced capacity at its flagship Ludwigshafen site, citing high energy costs, overcapacity, and weak demand, while simultaneously building a roughly 8.7 billion euro integrated Verbund complex in Zhanjiang, China, which its CEO Markus Kamieth presented as proof of what "the future of chemistry looks like: efficient, digital and sustainable by design" (BASF, 2026; bluewin/SDA, 2026).

To make matters worse, underneath these headlines lie structural and social issues. The consistent high energy prices after the loss of Russian pipeline gas, an ageing population, heavy bureaucracy, and a comparative lag in digital adoption are just a few large factors to mention (ifo Institute, 2024). The technical reading here is difficult to dispute, given that the old model really is under pressure. However, it is important to note that these shocks are concentrated in the most exposed, energy-intensive parts of the economy. They effect often the large legacy incumbents, such as flagship carmakers and bulk-chemical producers, rather than being spread evenly across all of German industry. Moreover, the fact that the pressure is so concentrated is precisely what the decline narrative tends to overlook. Even at Volkswagen, the eventual December 2024 settlement with the IG Metall union avoided outright plant closures, instead trimming capacity by more than 700,000 vehicles and cutting some 35,000 jobs by 2030, which is a painful restructuring but a managed adjustment rather than a collapse (electrive, 2026).

The Overlooked Resilience: Energy, Digitalization, and Trade

Yet, when looking at that same window of time, Germany has an adaptive capacity that the decline narrative tends to discount. One of the clearest ways to see this is in the energy sector itself. Renewable energies have risen to cover 54.1 percent of German gross electricity consumption in 2024, which was the second year in a row that more than half of consumption came from renewables, showing an increasing trend in contrast from 27.6 percent in 2014 and just 17.2 percent in 2010 (Umweltbundesamt, 2025; Quaschning, 2025). This demonstrates a restructuring of both the cost and the security profile of the system. However, the most striking aspect was not the volume but the speed. The LNG terminals at Wilhelmshaven, Brunsbüttel, and Lubmin and Mukran were planned and commissioned in months rather than the usual years. At the inauguration of the Lubmin terminal in January 2023, Chancellor Olaf Scholz noted that only "about half a year" had passed from application to completion, and he praised a wholly new "Deutschlandtempo," or Germany speed, for what the state proved capable of doing under genuine pressure (NDR, 2023; IEA, 2023). Off the Mecklenburg-Vorpommern coast, Baltic offshore wind projects such as Baltic Eagle now feed the 50Hertz transmission grid. This 476 megawatt wind farm, with fifty turbines connected via the Ostwind 2 link, was fully energised in July 2025 and, together with the neighbouring Wikinger farm, can meet around 45 percent of the state's total electricity consumption (Iberdrola, 2025). Meanwhile, the decommissioned site at Lubmin is being repositioned toward a hydrogen future, as the WAL ("Hydrogen from Lubmin") project plans 100 megawatts of electrolysis in its first phase, scalable to 600 megawatts, feeding the converted 400 kilometre Lubmin to Bobbau hydrogen pipeline that entered service in December 2025 and reuses the very infrastructure laid for Russian gas (H2APEX, 2026).

Germany's resilience is not limited to energy; it can be seen in digitalization as well. The very concept of Industrie 4.0, meaning the digital integration of manufacturing through sensors, data, and connected systems, originated in Germany. This idea remains a genuine strength when paired with the country's engineering base. On the Baltic coast of Germany this is not abstract, given that modern offshore wind farms depend on it to operate. As recent research puts it, "digital infrastructure based on the Industry 4.0 concept, such as [the] digital twin, enables data collection, visualization, and analysis of wind power analytic[s] at either individual turbine or wind farm level," supported by predictive-maintenance algorithms and standardised data exchange (Haghshenas et al., 2023). In other words, the energy transition and the information systems that manage it are now inseparable. This can lead to questions related to financial and information management, such as determining whether and in which way these assets are bankable. Germany also still retains frontier research capacity precisely where heavy industry never concentrated, as the Max Planck Institute's Wendelstein 7-X stellarator in Greifswald is among the world's leading nuclear fusion experiments. In May 2025 it set a world record for the fusion "triple product" sustained over a long plasma pulse of 43 seconds, a result its head of operations called "another important milestone on the way toward a power-plant-capable stellarator" (Max Planck Institute for Plasma Physics, 2025).

Changing global trade trends is the last area of this analysis. The BASF decision discussed above is often interpreted as an escape from high energy costs and weak European demand, but it is better understood as part of a broader "de-risking" of supply chains, in which firms diversify rather than abandon production. As European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen framed the strategy, "it is neither viable nor in Europe's interest to decouple from China ... this is why we need to focus on de-risk not de-couple," emphasising the defence of European interests, dialogue, and diversification (von der Leyen, 2023). Additionally, the export-oriented Mittelstand, what is often called the "Hidden Champions," continues to dominate narrow global market niches and runs substantial trade surpluses, which is difficult to reconcile with a story of uniform collapse. Germany hosts roughly 1,600 such firms, about half of the estimated 3,400 Hidden Champions worldwide, and in 2024 the United States overtook China as Germany's largest single trading partner, generating a record bilateral surplus of around 70 billion euros (Simon-Kucher, 2024; Economy Insights, 2025).

Conversely, one may argue that these aspects in regards to energy, digitalization, and global trade are too slow or weak to offset the decline in the large legacy sectors. While this counterargument is valid in some ways, such as that fusion and offshore wind will not replace automotive jobs overnight, it still underestimates how substantially examples like the Lubmin pivot has already changed the comparative position of an entire region. Therefore, these upcoming changes can be seen as a strengths in a reinvention that is already underway.

Overall, the energy transition, the digital systems that now run it, and the changes in global trade all indicate that Germany is rebuilding its industrial foundations rather than simply losing them. While there may be growing pains in the process, there are several examples of hope in even regions such as Mecklenburg-Vorpommern that are usually known for economic hardship.

Social Implications on the Outcome

Even so, this technical and economic capacity rests on a social foundation that the debates on Germany's status tends to ignore, and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern shows why the foundation is fragile. The state is a deindustrialised post-reunification periphery, and the depth of its disadvantage is easy to document. It records the lowest per-capita purchasing power of any federal state, at 23,312 euros in 2024 against a national average of 26,870 euros (Acxiom, 2024). It is, in the words of one report, "despite all political efforts ... [still] the low-wage state in Germany," where 36.1 percent of roughly 351,000 full-time employees earn less than 2,750 euros gross per month, the highest share of low earners in the country, and around 62 percent earn under 3,500 euros (Süddeutsche Zeitung, 2025). Its GDP per capita, near 37,600 euros, sits at the bottom of the league table alongside Saxony-Anhalt and Thuringia, far below the German average of about 50,860 euros (Statistisches Bundesamt, 2025). And although unemployment has fallen markedly, from 14.1 percent in 2008 to 7.9 percent in 2024, it remains the highest among the territorial states and well above the national figure (Statista, 2025). The region's demographic scars also run deep, since after 1990 "the eastern federal states experienced a wave of emigration, mainly of young people, especially women," which shrank the population and accelerated ageing (OSW, 2025). For example, consider the case of a young worker in a region that already feels left behind, how might this worker feel when asked to accept wind farms and pipelines whose benefits appear to flow somewhere else entirely? Political issues may also have implications in this situation. As Guriev and Papaioannou (2022) show in their synthesis of the economics of populism, deindustrialised communities have repeatedly "provided a fertile ground for populists," with support concentrating in regions of industrial decline. In Mecklenburg-Vorpommern the pattern is acute, since ahead of the state election scheduled for 20 September 2026 the right-wing populist Alternative für Deutschland (AfD) led polls at 36 to 38 percent, more than double its 2021 result and ahead of every governing party (NDR/Infratest dimap, 2026). This matters for industry, given that the transition which constitutes Germany's resilience is a decade-long and politically dependent project. Where regions such as Mecklenburg-Vorpommern become reservoirs of anti-transition sentiment, they can stall the very grid expansion and reforms on which the rest of industry depends. In this way, a "just transition" ceases to be a social add-on and becomes a precondition for resilience.

Strategic Priorities for Stakeholders

If Germany is going through a reinvention rather than a straight-forward decline, the strategic task for stakeholders is to remove the constraints that hinder this change. For policymakers, the priorities are largely physical and political. For example, a great strategy would involve resolving the north-to-south grid bottleneck so that renewable-rich areas such as Mecklenburg-Vorpommern can host, rather than export, energy-intensive demand (Bundesnetzagentur, 2023). Second, they should focus on policies and plans to deliver a competitive industrial power price and scale hydrogen infrastructure, for which the Baltic coast, already the start of the FLOW north to south hydrogen axis, could be used as a natural hub (H2APEX, 2026). As a final suggestion, policymakers should consider cutting permitting bureaucracy, since the LNG buildout proved this is possible, and pair skilled-migration reform with investments in lagging regions, which the populism literature suggests is among the most effective ways to blunt anti-transition politics (Guriev & Papaioannou, 2022).

For companies, the priorities are digital and strategic. Firms should invest in the digital systems that convert renewable abundance into competitive advantage, such as digital twins, AI-driven energy optimisation, and predictive maintenance, which is precisely where information management meets industrial finance (Haghshenas et al., 2023). In addition, they should continue to diversify the risk of supply chains rather than depending on any single market, following the de-risking logic now embedded in EU policy (von der Leyen, 2023). Businesses could emulate the focused, export-led Mittelstand model rather than chase scale in commoditised segments (Simon-Kucher, 2024). Where firms site energy assets in peripheries, they should act as embedded local stakeholders rather than absentee operators, which directly addresses the social constraint above.

Conclusion: Reinvention of Germany

First, when examining the headline statistics, it can be seen that the pessimistic case is real but concentrated. This is apparent as the pressure seems to fall on legacy, energy-intensive incumbents rather than on the whole of German industry, and even there it has so far produced managed restructuring rather than collapse. Additionally, the overlooked strengths are equally real, since renewable generation exceeding half of electricity consumption, the digital systems that manage it, the reinvention of trade through de-risking, and frontier research such as Wendelstein 7-X all reveal that Germany is a country that can adapt quickly. However, one counterargument may be that these strengths are too small or too slow to develop to stand against the decline of the large sectors. While it is true that they will not replace legacy jobs overnight, this objection misses how much the reinvention has already changed a region such as Mecklenburg-Vorpommern. Essentially, the question of whether German industry declines depends less on engineering, which remains world-class, than on whether the grid, the price of industrial energy, digital adoption, and the trust of Germany's communities can keep pace with the transition. On the whole, the Lubmin example explored in this essay shows how Germany can transform in the face of obstacles. This case demonstrates that the German industry is not in a straightforward decline but in a contested reinvention whose outcome is currently being decided.
